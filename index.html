Why EV shoppers configure a car but never drive it

Shoppers on online-first EV brands finish a configuration, then leave without booking a test drive. I looked at where they drop off, why, and what a product team should build first.

The problem

The test drive is where EV doubts get settled, and too few shoppers reach it

Most buyers in Germany still drive before they buy. For electric cars the drive matters even more, because it answers questions about range, charging and comfort that a spec sheet cannot. Yet the step from a finished configuration to a booked drive often asks for a form, a phone number and a wait for a call-back.

Where it breaks

Two different leaks after the summary screen

The first leak is about readiness: people leave to check monthly cost and compare models, and need an easy way back. The second is friction: they were ready, but had to hand over a phone number or wait for a call before they could see whether Saturday was free.

I also reviewed how Tesla, Polestar, BYD and smart handle booking. Tesla is fastest, with booking in under a minute and self-serve drives, but has few sites. Polestar offers flexible drive lengths and home drives, but has few locations. BYD and smart depend on each dealer or agent to respond, which is where public complaints cluster.

Method note: the user conversations were informal and written up afterwards, so remarks are paraphrased. Structured interviews with a fixed guide are under way to validate the findings.

Key insight

Forums only show failures. Conversations showed that many shoppers simply aren't ready yet.

Not one forum post mentioned missing price or range information, yet 6 of 8 people needed it before they would drive. People post when something breaks, not when they quietly leave to think. Relying on reviews alone would have led to a booking-only fix and missed half the problem.

User stories from the PRD

US1

As a shopper who just configured, I see bookable times on the summary screen.

The 3 earliest slots within 50 km show, matched to my model. No empty list: if nothing is free in 14 days, I see the next location and "notify me".
US2

As a shopper, I book with as little typing as possible.

Four fields, no account, configuration attached automatically, marketing consent unticked and separate (GDPR). Median under 60 seconds in usability tests.
US3

As a shopper, I know it's confirmed right away.

Confirmation, email and SMS within 1 minute. A call only if I asked for one. Host cancellations reach me within 2 hours, with 3 new slots.
US4

As a shopper, I can change plans in one tap.

Reminders at 24 h and 2 h carry reschedule and cancel links. A cancelled slot is released within 5 minutes.
US5

As a location host, I get the booking with the configuration.

Booking reaches the host's calendar within 5 minutes. Unacknowledged after 4 working hours, the regional manager is alerted.

Out of scope for the MVP: home and self-serve drives, fleet approval workflows, financing and trade-in, configurator redesign.

Success

One number to move, one that must not break

North starConfigurator-to-test-drive booking rate

Drives booked within 7 days ÷ completed configurations. Target: +20% relative vs control.

GuardrailTest-drive show-up rate

Drives taken ÷ drives booked. Easier booking must not buy no-shows: no drop over 3 percentage points.

Measured with a 4-week A/B test on the summary screen, counted per configuration rather than per session. Supporting metrics: time to confirmation, slot coverage by postcode and drive-to-order rate after 60 days.

What I'd test first with real data

The riskiest assumptions

  1. Locations can publish live slots. If they can't, the MVP becomes a request form with a faster call-back. First test: ask three hosts how they schedule drives today.
  2. Friction, not low intent, drives the drop-off. The conversations say it's both. Funnel data would show how many click "book" but don't submit.
  3. Easier booking won't raise no-shows. Watch the guardrail weekly during the A/B test.
